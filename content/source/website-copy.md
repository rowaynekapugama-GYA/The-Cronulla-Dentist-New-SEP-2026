### How we approach dentistry

Three things shape how appointments run here.

**Explanation before treatment.** You should understand what is happening in your mouth before anyone starts working on it. We will show you what we have found, describe the options in plain language, and include the option of waiting. What you decide after that is genuinely your call.

### What happens at a check-up

A general check-up at our Cronulla practice is unhurried by design. Rushing this appointment defeats its purpose.

**We look, properly.** An examination of the teeth, gums, tongue, cheeks, palate and the soft tissues of the mouth, including a check for anything unusual that warrants further attention.

**X-rays where indicated.** Some decay develops between teeth or under existing fillings where it simply cannot be seen. Digital X-rays use a low radiation dose and are taken when clinically appropriate rather than automatically at every visit.

**A professional clean.** Scaling to remove hardened plaque that brushing cannot shift, followed by polishing. Fluoride application where it is likely to help.

**A conversation.** We tell you what we found, what it means, and what your options are. Including doing nothing for now, which is sometimes a reasonable choice and will be presented as one when it is.

You will not be handed a treatment plan and asked to commit on the spot.

### How often should you come in

The standard advice is every six months, and for most people that works. It is not universal.

Patients with a history of gum disease, a high decay rate, dry mouth, certain medical conditions or extensive existing dental work may benefit from more frequent visits. Others with consistently healthy results and good home care can sometimes stretch the interval.

We will suggest a schedule based on what we actually see in your mouth, and we will explain the reasoning.

### What general dentistry covers here

**Fillings.** Tooth coloured composite fillings that bond to the tooth structure and match the surrounding enamel. Used for decay, small chips and worn areas.

**Wisdom teeth.** Assessment of whether wisdom teeth have room to come through, and removal where they are impacted, causing crowding, or repeatedly becoming infected. Straightforward cases are handled here. More complex cases may warrant referral, and we will tell you honestly which category yours falls into.

**Mouth and night guards.** Custom made appliances for grinding and clenching, and protective mouthguards for contact sport. Given how much junior sport is played across Woolooware and Cronulla, custom sports guards come up regularly. They fit better and protect better than the boil and bite versions sold over the counter.

**Extractions.** When a tooth cannot reasonably be saved, removing it carefully and discussing what comes next.

**Preventive advice.** Practical, specific and based on your mouth rather than a leaflet. If a particular area keeps developing decay, we will work out why.

### If it has been a while

A meaningful number of people have not seen a dentist in years, and the reason is usually embarrassment about how long it has been. That embarrassment then makes the gap longer.

We want to be direct about this. Nobody at this practice will lecture you about the years you missed. It is not useful, it is not kind, and it makes people less likely to come back. We are interested in your mouth as it is today.

If you would prefer to start with a conversation rather than an examination, that appointment is available.

### When should a child first see a dentist

Earlier than most parents expect. The general guidance is around the first birthday, or within six months of the first tooth appearing.

At that age there is very little to examine. That is precisely the point. The first visit becomes a low pressure introduction, a look in the mouth, a conversation with you about brushing and diet, and nothing that could unsettle them. The dentist becomes an ordinary place before there is any reason for it to be otherwise.

### How we run a first visit

Short, unhurried and predictable.

**They can look first.** The chair goes up and down. The little mirror gets handled. The suction makes a noise. Children cope far better with equipment they have already inspected.

**We narrate everything.** No surprises. We say what we are about to do before we do it, in words appropriate to their age.

**You stay close.** For younger children, a parent in the room is usually helpful.

**We stop when it is not working.** A visit where a three year old sits in the chair and lets us count four teeth is a successful visit. Forcing the remaining sixteen teaches them the dentist is somewhere they lose control. We would rather see them again in a few weeks and get further.

### What children’s dentistry covers

**Check-ups and cleans**, adjusted for age and cooperation.

**Fissure sealants.** A protective coating painted into the deep grooves of newly erupted adult molars, where food and bacteria collect and brushing struggles to reach. Quick, painless and one of the more effective preventive measures available for children.

**Fluoride applications**, where clinically indicated.

**Fillings** for decay in baby teeth. This surprises some parents, who assume a tooth destined to fall out does not need treating. Baby teeth hold space for the adult teeth behind them and untreated decay can cause pain, infection and problems with the adult tooth developing underneath.

**Monitoring of growth and alignment.** We keep an eye on how the jaw and adult teeth are developing and will raise orthodontic assessment at the appropriate point if it looks worthwhile.

**Mouthguards for sport.** With the amount of junior football, netball and rugby league played around Woolooware and Cronulla, custom fitted mouthguards come up often. They fit better, stay in place better and are more comfortable than over the counter versions, which means children actually wear them.

### The Child Dental Benefits Schedule

The CDBS is a federal government scheme that can cover a portion of dental treatment for eligible children.

Broadly, it applies to children aged between 0 and 17 for at least one day of the calendar year, where the family receives certain government payments such as Family Tax Benefit Part A. Eligible children have a capped benefit amount available over a two year period, covering services including examinations, X-rays, cleans, fissure sealants, fillings and extractions.

Eligibility is determined by Services Australia rather than by us, and families are usually notified directly. If you are unsure, we can check your child’s eligibility when you book.

### Helping at home

The habits that matter are unremarkable and consistent.

Brush twice a day with a fluoride toothpaste appropriate for their age. Supervise brushing until around eight, because coordination for a thorough clean develops later than most people assume. Watch the frequency of sugar rather than only the quantity, since repeated small exposures across a day are harder on enamel than a single serve. Keep sweet drinks away from bedtime.

And keep your own dread out of it. Children read adult anxiety accurately. Describing a check-up as something to get through tells them there is something to fear.

### Starting with the least invasive option

There is a hierarchy in cosmetic dentistry, and it runs from reversible to permanent.

Whitening changes nothing structurally. Bonding adds material without removing much, if any. Reshaping removes a very small amount of enamel. Veneers require permanent preparation of the tooth. Crowns involve more again.

Our approach is to begin at the conservative end and only move up when there is a reason to.

A patient convinced they need eight veneers sometimes needs whitening and the reshaping of one edge. Someone unhappy with a gap may be a candidate for bonding rather than porcelain. Not always. But it is worth establishing before committing to work that cannot be undone.

We will always tell you what the more conservative option would achieve, even where it earns the practice less.

### What we offer

**Teeth whitening.** In chair and take home options. The most common starting point, and often enough on its own. Worth knowing before you begin: whitening lifts natural tooth structure but does not change the colour of existing crowns, veneers or fillings, which can leave older restorations looking noticeably darker afterwards.

**Dental veneers.** Thin custom shells bonded to the front surface of the teeth, used to change shape, colour, spacing or apparent alignment. Effective, and a genuine commitment, since preparing a tooth for a veneer usually alters it permanently.

### Dental crowns

#### When a crown is the right answer

A filling replaces missing tooth structure. A crown covers what remains and holds it together, which is a different job.

Crowns are typically recommended when:

**A tooth has cracked.** A cracked tooth flexes under chewing pressure and the crack propagates. A crown holds it together and stops that movement.

**Decay has been extensive.** Once enough tooth structure is gone, a large filling becomes the weak point rather than the fix. Fillings do not strengthen a tooth, they fill a space.

**A tooth has had root canal therapy.** Back teeth in particular become more brittle after root canal treatment and are prone to fracturing under load. A crown is usually recommended to protect them.

**A tooth is heavily worn.** From grinding, acid erosion or decades of ordinary use.

**A tooth is both damaged and visible.** In which case the crown addresses structure and appearance at once.

#### Materials

**All ceramic and zirconia** crowns are the usual choice today. They look natural, are strong enough for most positions including molars, and contain no metal.

**Porcelain fused to metal** has a metal substructure for strength with porcelain over it. Very durable, though a dark line can eventually appear at the gum margin as gums recede.

**Gold** is rarely requested now but remains an excellent material for back teeth. It wears at a rate similar to natural enamel and is gentle on the opposing tooth.

We will recommend based on the position of the tooth, how much force it takes and how visible it is.

Two appointments in most cases.

At the second, usually two to three weeks later, the permanent crown is trialled, adjusted, checked against your bite and cemented in place.

### Dental bridges

#### How a bridge works

A bridge replaces one or more missing teeth with a fixed unit. The teeth either side of the gap, called abutment teeth, are prepared for crowns, and the replacement tooth is joined between them. The whole assembly is cemented in place and is not removable.

#### When a bridge makes sense

Bridges suit patients who want a fixed replacement without surgery, who are not candidates for implants, or whose neighbouring teeth already need crowns for their own reasons. In that last case a bridge is particularly logical, because those teeth were going to be prepared anyway.

#### The trade-off worth understanding

A bridge requires permanently reducing the healthy teeth on either side of the gap. If those teeth are sound and untouched, this is a real cost that deserves weighing against an implant, which leaves them alone.

We will lay out that comparison honestly, including cost, timeframe and long term implications, rather than steering you toward one option.

Similar to a crown. Preparation of the abutment teeth, impressions or a scan, a temporary bridge, then fitting of the permanent one a few weeks later.

Well made crowns and bridges commonly last many years, and often well over a decade. That is a range, not a promise.

### If a tooth has been knocked out

This is the one where what you do in the first half hour genuinely changes the outcome, so it is worth reading before you ever need it.

### What gingivitis is

Plaque forms constantly on teeth. Where it is left along the gum line, the bacteria within it trigger an inflammatory response in the surrounding tissue.

That response is gingivitis. The gums become inflamed, swollen and prone to bleeding. Importantly, at this stage the inflammation is confined to the gum tissue itself. The bone and the fibres holding the tooth in place have not been affected.

That distinction is everything. Gum tissue recovers. Bone does not. Treat gingivitis and the gums return to health with no lasting damage. Leave it, and in some people it progresses to periodontitis, where bone loss begins and the damage becomes permanent.

Not everyone with gingivitis progresses. But there is no reliable way to know in advance who will.

### Signs to look for

- Gums that bleed when brushing or cleaning between teeth

- Redness along the gum margin, where healthy gums are usually a paler pink

- Puffiness or swelling of the gum between the teeth

- Tenderness

- Persistent bad breath or an unpleasant taste

- Blood on the pillow or a metallic taste in the morning

Gingivitis does not usually hurt, which is precisely why it is overlooked. Bleeding is the signal that matters.

### What makes it more likely

**Inconsistent cleaning between teeth.** The most common contributor by a distance. Brushing reaches roughly three of the five surfaces of a tooth. The two surfaces facing the neighbouring teeth are where gum inflammation most often starts.

**Smoking.** Complicates matters, because smoking reduces blood flow to the gums and can mask the bleeding that would otherwise warn you. Smokers can have significant gum disease with very little bleeding.

**Hormonal change.** Pregnancy, puberty and the menopause all alter how gum tissue responds to plaque. Pregnancy gingivitis is common and worth attention rather than dismissal.

**Diabetes**, particularly where blood glucose is not well controlled.

**Some medications**, including those causing dry mouth and certain blood pressure and epilepsy medications that can cause gum overgrowth.

**Crowded teeth, ill fitting restorations and orthodontic appliances**, all of which create areas that are harder to clean.

**Stress**, which affects immune response and often coincides with a drop in oral hygiene routines.

### Treating it

Treatment is unglamorous and usually effective.

**A professional clean** to remove the plaque and hardened calculus the toothbrush cannot reach. This is the part you cannot do yourself.

**Identifying the specific problem areas.** Gingivitis is rarely uniform across the whole mouth. It concentrates where cleaning is hardest. We work out where your trouble spots are and address those, rather than issuing general advice.

**Adjusting technique and tools.** Often the change needed is small. The right size interdental brush for the gaps you actually have. A different angle at the gum line. An electric brush if you tend to scrub.

**Addressing contributing factors** where we can, including reviewing restorations with overhanging margins that trap plaque.

**A review appointment** a few weeks later to confirm the inflammation has resolved. This matters. Improvement that is assumed rather than checked is how gingivitis quietly persists.

With consistent home care, gums usually settle within a couple of weeks. Expect bleeding to increase slightly for the first few days as you start cleaning inflamed areas properly. That is normal, and it settles. It is not a reason to stop.

### When it is more than gingivitis

If gum recession has begun, if teeth feel loose or have shifted, if there is a bad taste that will not clear, or if there is pus around the gum, the situation has likely moved beyond gingivitis. That needs assessment rather than a better brushing routine.

Our periodontal treatment and gum disease pages cover what happens then.

### What gum disease actually is

Teeth are not fixed rigidly into the jaw. Each one is held by a ligament attached to the surrounding bone, with the gum forming a seal around the top.

In periodontitis, bacteria below the gum line trigger a sustained inflammatory response. Over time, that response destroys the ligament fibres and the bone supporting the tooth. The gum detaches from the tooth surface, forming a deepening space called a pocket. Pockets are harder to clean than the open tooth surface, so bacteria accumulate further, and the process continues.

The critical point is that the bone lost does not come back. Treatment stops the process and stabilises what remains. It does not rebuild what has gone, except in a limited set of cases where regenerative techniques are appropriate.

This is why early diagnosis matters so much more here than in most areas of dentistry.

### Gingivitis and periodontitis are not the same thing

Gingivitis is inflammation confined to the gum tissue. It is reversible and treated straightforwardly, and it has its own page.

Periodontitis is what some cases of gingivitis progress to. Once bone involvement begins, the condition changes character. It becomes something to be managed long term rather than cured.

Most people with gingivitis never progress. Susceptibility varies considerably between individuals, and genetics plays a real part, which is why two people with similar oral hygiene can have very different gum health.

**Pocket depth measurements.** A fine probe measures the space between gum and tooth at several points around each tooth. Up to about three millimetres is normal. Deeper readings indicate attachment loss. These numbers are recorded so we can compare them over time.

**X-rays**, which show the bone level directly and reveal loss that cannot be seen or felt.

**Recession, mobility and furcation involvement**, meaning bone loss reaching the point where the roots of a multi-rooted tooth divide.

**Contributing factors**, including smoking, diabetes, medications, family history and stress.

A full periodontal assessment takes time and produces a chart rather than an impression. That chart is what treatment planning is based on.

- Gums that bleed when brushing or cleaning between teeth

### Stage one: non-surgical periodontal therapy

Also called root surface debridement, or deep cleaning.

A routine hygiene appointment cleans the tooth surfaces you can see and just below the gum margin. Periodontal therapy goes further, cleaning the root surfaces inside the pockets that have formed, where the bacteria driving the disease are living.

**How it is done.** Under local anaesthetic, so it is comfortable. Ultrasonic and hand instruments are used to remove deposits and disrupt the bacterial film on the root surfaces.

**How long it takes.** Usually two to four appointments, working through the mouth in sections. Treating one quadrant at a time means the anaesthetic is localised and you are not numb across your whole mouth for an afternoon.

**Afterwards.** Expect some tenderness for a few days and increased sensitivity to cold, which typically settles over a few weeks. You may notice gums appearing to recede slightly. This is not the treatment causing damage. It is swelling reducing, revealing where the gum line actually sits now that the inflammation has gone. Some patients notice small gaps opening between teeth for the same reason.

This stage resolves or substantially improves most cases.

### Stage two: reassessment

Usually six to twelve weeks after the last treatment appointment.

We measure the same points as before and compare. Pockets that have reduced and stopped bleeding indicate the tissue has reattached and the disease is stable. Pockets that remain deep and continue to bleed indicate ongoing activity.

This appointment is the one patients most often skip, because by this point their mouth feels considerably better. Feeling better is not the same as being stable. The measurements are what tell us whether the treatment worked, and without them the next decision is guesswork.

### Stage three: further treatment where needed

Where deep pockets persist despite thorough non-surgical treatment, options include repeating debridement in the specific areas still active, or referral to a periodontist for surgical treatment.

Surgical periodontal treatment allows direct access to root surfaces that cannot be reached through a pocket, reshaping of bone contours, and in selected cases regenerative procedures using grafting materials.

We will refer where referral genuinely serves you better, and we will explain why rather than simply handing over a name.

Adjunctive antibiotics are used in some circumstances, though they do not substitute for mechanical cleaning of the root surfaces and are not routinely required.

### Stage four: maintenance, indefinitely

This is the stage that determines the long term outcome, and it is the one most likely to be neglected.

Periodontitis is a chronic condition. Treatment stabilises it. The bacteria recolonise the pockets over a matter of months, and without regular professional cleaning below the gum line the disease resumes.

**Maintenance appointments are typically every three to four months**, not six. That interval is based on how quickly bacterial populations re-establish in previously diseased pockets.

Each maintenance visit includes cleaning below the gum line, remeasurement of pocket depths, and review of any sites showing change. Intervals can be extended over time where readings remain consistently stable.

The research on this is consistent. Patients who attend maintenance retain their teeth at considerably higher rates than those who complete active treatment and then stop. If you take one thing from this page, take that.

**It can** stop or slow the progression of the disease, reduce pocket depths, eliminate inflammation and bleeding, and give you a stable situation you can maintain for many years.

**It cannot** regrow bone that has already been lost, except in limited cases suitable for regenerative techniques. Teeth that have lost most of their support will not become firm again.

### Why a tooth needs one

Inside every tooth is a chamber containing nerve tissue and blood vessels, running down through the roots. When bacteria reach that space, the tissue becomes inflamed and then infected.

It gets there through deep decay, a crack, trauma to the tooth, or occasionally through repeated dental work over many years.

Once the nerve tissue inside a tooth is infected, it does not recover. The body cannot clear an infection in that space, because there is no meaningful blood supply left to deliver an immune response. Antibiotics may settle the surrounding swelling temporarily, but they do not resolve what is happening inside the tooth. The infection then spreads out of the root tip into the surrounding bone, forming an abscess.

At that point there are two options. Remove the tooth, or remove the infected tissue from inside it and seal it. The second is root canal therapy.

### Signs you may need one

- Severe or throbbing toothache, particularly pain that wakes you at night

- Prolonged sensitivity to heat, or pain that lingers well after a hot or cold stimulus is removed

- Pain when biting or applying pressure to a particular tooth

- A tooth that has darkened compared with its neighbours

- Swelling of the gum or face

- A pimple like spot on the gum near a tooth, which may discharge and taste unpleasant

Not all of these appear in every case. Some teeth with dead nerves cause no symptoms at all and are found on a routine X-ray, which is one reason those X-rays are taken.

If the pain stops suddenly on its own, that is not reassurance. It often means the nerve has died. The infection continues regardless.

### What the procedure involves

**Anaesthetic first**, and we take the time to confirm you are properly numb before starting. Teeth with active infection can be harder to numb, and if the first approach is not working there are alternatives. Tell us rather than tolerating it.

**Access and cleaning.** An opening is made through the top of the tooth. The infected tissue is removed from the canals, which are then cleaned, shaped and disinfected. This is the part that takes time, particularly on molars, which have several canals of varying shape.

**Filling and sealing.** The canals are filled with an inert material and sealed to prevent bacteria re-entering.

**Restoration.** The access opening is restored, and in most cases, particularly on back teeth, a crown is recommended. A root treated tooth has lost internal structure and is more prone to fracturing under chewing load. The crown is not an optional extra, it is usually what determines whether the tooth survives long term.

Treatment is completed over one to three appointments depending on the tooth and the extent of infection.

Root canal treatment has good long term success rates when the tooth is properly restored afterwards and maintained. It is not guaranteed. The alternative to root canal therapy is extraction, which is faster and cheaper up front. It then leaves a gap, and replacing a tooth with an implant or bridge generally costs more than saving it would have.

**Cysts.** Occasionally a cyst develops around an unerupted tooth, which requires attention.

**Gum problems** in the area that cannot be resolved while the tooth remains.

### When we leave them alone

If a wisdom tooth is fully through, in reasonable alignment, cleanable and causing no problems, monitoring is usually the right approach.

Removing wisdom teeth is not risk free, and there is no benefit in taking out a tooth that is doing its job. Preventive removal of symptom free, fully erupted wisdom teeth is not routinely recommended.

We will keep an eye on them at your check-ups and take X-rays periodically to see whether anything has changed.

### Assessment

Assessment starts with an X-ray, usually a panoramic image showing all four wisdom teeth, their angulation, root shape and their relationship to surrounding structures.

The important relationship is with the inferior alveolar nerve, which runs through the lower jaw and supplies sensation to the lip and chin. Lower wisdom tooth roots sometimes sit close to it. Where the X-ray suggests they are very close, a 3D scan may be recommended for a clearer picture before proceeding.

This assessment determines both the difficulty of the extraction and where it should be done.

### Where treatment happens

**Straightforward cases** are commonly handled in the practice under local anaesthetic.

**More complex cases** are referred to an oral and maxillofacial surgeon, sometimes for treatment in hospital under general anaesthetic. Deep impactions, roots close to the nerve, and cases where all four are being removed at once are typical reasons.

We will tell you which category yours falls into at the assessment, before you commit to anything. Attempting a difficult extraction in the wrong setting serves nobody.

### What the procedure involves

Under local anaesthetic, the area is fully numbed. Where the tooth is covered by gum, a small incision is made to expose it. Some bone may need to be removed, and the tooth is often sectioned into pieces, which allows it to come out through a smaller opening than it would otherwise need. Stitches are frequently placed, and dissolving stitches are common.

You will feel pressure and movement. You should not feel pain. Say something if you do.

### Recovery

**The first 24 hours.** Bite firmly on the gauze provided. Do not rinse, spit forcefully, smoke or use a straw, as these can dislodge the clot. Rest, keep your head elevated, and use a cold compress on the outside of the cheek.

**Swelling** typically peaks around day two or three and then subsides. Bruising is possible.

**Pain** is usually manageable with over the counter pain relief taken as directed. Take it before the anaesthetic wears off rather than waiting.

**Eating.** Soft, cool foods initially, building up as comfort allows.

**From day two**, gentle rinsing with warm salt water several times daily.

**Most people** take a couple of days off work or study, longer for more involved extractions.

### Risks worth knowing

**Dry socket**, where the clot is lost and the socket is exposed, causing significant pain typically three to five days afterwards. It is treatable and we will see you promptly if it happens. Smoking substantially increases the risk.

**Infection**, uncommon but possible.

**Nerve disturbance.** With lower wisdom teeth there is a risk of numbness or altered sensation in the lip, chin or tongue. It is usually temporary. Permanent change is uncommon but possible, and the risk is assessed from your imaging and discussed with you beforehand.

**Jaw stiffness and difficulty opening** for several days.

### Sports mouthguards

The Shire plays a lot of sport. Between junior league at Woolooware, the football and netball clubs across Cronulla and Caringbah, and everything played on weekends across Greenhills Beach and Burraneer, dental injuries from sport are a regular presentation.

A mouthguard absorbs and distributes the force of a knock. It protects the teeth from being chipped, broken or knocked out, protects the lips and cheeks from being cut against the teeth, and reduces the risk of jaw injury.

**Custom versus over the counter.** The boil and bite guards sold in sports stores are better than nothing, and that is roughly the extent of the endorsement they deserve.

They are moulded by biting into softened plastic, which produces an approximate fit. They tend to be bulky, which interferes with breathing and speech. They loosen and need holding in place with the teeth, which is why so many end up being carried in a hand or a pocket during play. And a mouthguard that is not being worn provides no protection at all.

A custom guard is made from an impression or digital scan of your teeth. It fits closely, stays put without being held, allows normal speech and breathing, and is thicker in the areas that take impact and thinner where they do not. Children and teenagers wear them because they are not unpleasant to wear.

**For growing mouths**, a custom guard will need replacing as teeth come through and the jaw develops. Check the fit at the start of each season.

**With braces**, a mouthguard is more important rather than less, and needs to be made specifically to accommodate the appliance.

Guards can be made in club colours, which sounds trivial and is a genuinely useful way of getting a teenager to wear one.

### Night guards for grinding and clenching

Grinding and clenching, known as bruxism, mostly happens during sleep, which is why many people have no idea they do it until damage appears or a partner mentions the noise.

**Signs to look for:**

- Teeth that are visibly flattened, chipped or worn at the edges

- Jaw ache or facial muscle soreness, particularly on waking

- Headaches around the temples in the morning

- Increased tooth sensitivity

- Clicking, popping or discomfort in the jaw joint

- Ridging along the inside of the cheeks or scalloped edges on the tongue

- A partner who hears grinding at night

**Why it matters.** The forces involved in nocturnal grinding are substantially higher than normal chewing forces and are sustained for far longer. Over years this wears through enamel, cracks teeth, causes fillings and crowns to fail, and contributes to jaw joint problems. Enamel does not regenerate, so wear is cumulative and permanent.

**What a night guard does.** It does not stop you grinding. It is important to be clear about that. What it does is place a hard acrylic layer between your upper and lower teeth so the wear happens to the guard rather than to your teeth, and it changes how the jaw muscles load, which many patients find reduces morning soreness.

The guard wears down over time and needs replacing periodically. That is the appliance doing its job.

**Custom fit matters here too.** Over the counter night guards are soft, which can encourage more chewing activity rather than less, and a poorly fitting guard can allow teeth to shift. A custom guard is made from a scan or impression, adjusted against your bite at the fitting, and reviewed.

**Causes.** Bruxism is associated with stress, sleep disturbance, some medications, alcohol and caffeine, and bite irregularities. A guard protects the teeth. Where an underlying cause can be identified and addressed, that is worth doing alongside it, and where sleep disordered breathing may be involved, medical assessment is appropriate.

Two appointments. A scan or impression at the first, and fitting and adjustment two to three weeks later. Bring the guard to your check-ups so we can assess wear and fit.

### Dentists do not diagnose sleep apnoea

We want to be direct about this, because a good deal of advertising in this area blurs it.

Obstructive sleep apnoea is diagnosed by a medical practitioner, usually on the basis of a sleep study, which may be conducted at home or in a laboratory. The study measures how often your breathing is interrupted, how far your oxygen levels fall and how fragmented your sleep is. Those measurements determine the severity of the condition and what treatment is appropriate.

What we can do is recognise signs that warrant investigation, refer you to your GP or a sleep physician, and provide an oral appliance where one has been indicated as part of your treatment.

What we cannot do is tell you whether you have sleep apnoea, or how severe it is, by looking in your mouth.

### Signs worth raising with your doctor

- Loud, persistent snoring, particularly with pauses in breathing

- Someone observing that you stop breathing, gasp or choke during sleep

- Waking unrefreshed despite an adequate night in bed

- Daytime sleepiness, especially falling asleep unintentionally

- Morning headaches

- Difficulty concentrating, memory problems or irritability

- Waking frequently, or needing to urinate repeatedly overnight

- A dry mouth or sore throat on waking

If you recognise several of these, particularly witnessed pauses in breathing or daytime sleepiness, see your GP. If you fall asleep while driving, treat that as urgent.

### What an oral appliance does

A mandibular advancement splint is a custom made device worn during sleep. It holds the lower jaw slightly forward, which increases the space at the back of the throat and reduces the tendency of the airway to collapse.

**For snoring** without sleep apnoea, an appliance can reduce or eliminate the noise for many people.

**For obstructive sleep apnoea**, appliances are an established treatment option, generally recommended for mild to moderate cases, and for patients with more severe disease who cannot tolerate CPAP. CPAP, which delivers pressurised air through a mask, is usually the first line treatment for moderate to severe sleep apnoea and is more effective at controlling it. Some patients manage well with CPAP. Others cannot tolerate it, and for them an appliance that is worn is more useful than a machine that is not.

Which is appropriate for you is a decision made with your doctor, informed by your sleep study, not a decision made in a dental chair.

### How it works here

**Referral first.** If you have not been assessed, we will refer you to your GP or a sleep physician before making anything. Fitting an appliance for snoring in someone with undiagnosed severe sleep apnoea risks masking the noise while the underlying condition continues untreated. That is the central reason we insist on this sequence.

**Dental assessment.** An appliance places sustained force on the teeth and jaw joint, so we assess whether your teeth, gums and jaw joint can tolerate it. Insufficient teeth, active gum disease or existing jaw joint problems may make an appliance unsuitable.

**Fitting and titration.** The appliance is made from scans or impressions and fitted, then adjusted gradually over several weeks to find the position that works with the least advancement necessary.

**Medical follow up.** Where an appliance is treating diagnosed sleep apnoea, a follow up sleep study is generally recommended to confirm it is actually controlling the condition. Feeling better is encouraging but is not the same as objective control.

**Ongoing dental review** to monitor tooth position and jaw joint comfort.

### Side effects to expect

Common, and mostly manageable.

Jaw or muscle soreness on waking, particularly early on. Increased saliva, or a dry mouth. Tooth tenderness. A temporary change in how the teeth meet in the morning, which usually settles within a short time of removing the appliance.

The longer term consideration is that appliance use can produce gradual changes in tooth position and bite over years of wear. This is well documented, it is usually minor, and it is one of the reasons ongoing dental review is part of treatment rather than optional.
